# Chapter 2 — Literature Review

## 2.0 Literature review method (search, screening, and inclusion criteria)

The literature review was conducted as a structured narrative review with an explicit screening protocol, rather than as a full PRISMA systematic review (which would exceed MSc time and scope). The goal was to establish what is known about (i) data-driven building energy prediction, especially from EPC-like tabular certificates; (ii) privacy-preserving / federated learning for energy and building data; (iii) explainable AI for tabular and building-energy models; and (iv) whether these strands have been jointly evaluated for UK high-rise stock.

**Search venues and strings.** Queries were run between April and August 2026 on Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, MDPI, SpringerLink, and arXiv. Core Boolean combinations included: `"federated learning" AND ("building energy" OR EPC OR "energy performance")`; `"explainable AI" OR SHAP OR LIME AND ("building energy" OR retrofit)`; `"Energy Performance Certificate" AND (machine learning OR XGBoost OR LightGBM)`; and `FedAvg OR FedProx AND energy`. Forward and backward citation chaining from seed reviews (Amasyali and El-Gohary, 2018; Wei et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2023) was used to recover additional primary studies.

**Inclusion criteria.** A source was included if it: (a) was peer-reviewed (journal or conference) or a widely cited preprint with clear methods; (b) addressed building energy prediction, load/energy forecasting for buildings or grids, federated learning for energy data, or XAI applied to energy models; (c) reported empirical results or a substantial methodological contribution; and (d) was published primarily between 2016 and 2026, with deliberate oversampling of 2024–2026 work to capture the rapid growth of FL+XAI in energy systems.

**Exclusion criteria.** Sources were excluded if they: (a) focused solely on physics simulation without a data-driven component; (b) were non-scholarly blog posts or vendor white papers without reproducible methods; (c) addressed only cybersecurity of smart meters without prediction or XAI; (d) duplicated an already included study (same experiment republished); or (e) lacked sufficient methodological detail to assess relevance to RQs.

**Shortlisting.** From an initial longlist of approximately 90 candidate titles/abstracts, about 40 were read in full. Roughly 25–30 are cited in this chapter as the core evidence base, prioritising (i) review papers that map the field, (ii) FL studies on building or residential energy with geographic or organisational silos, and (iii) SHAP/LIME applications to building energy models. Where multiple papers made similar claims, the more recent or more methodologically transparent study was preferred.

**Limitations of the review.** Grey literature and non-English sources were not systematically searched. The review is narrative and thematic, not a meta-analysis of effect sizes. These choices are appropriate for an MSc dissertation but mean the claim of “no prior UK high-rise EPC FL+XAI study” should be read as “none identified under this protocol,” not as a proof of absolute absence.

## 2.1 Building energy performance and EPC data

Building energy prediction research spans physics-based simulation, statistical regression, and modern machine learning. Physics-based tools (for example Dynamic Simulation and reduced-order thermal models) offer interpretability grounded in heat transfer but require detailed geometry and schedules that are rarely available at national scale. Data-driven approaches instead learn mappings from observable building descriptors to energy intensity or demand. Ensemble tree methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM) consistently perform well on tabular building datasets because they capture non-linear interactions among fabric, systems, and categorical typology fields without heavy feature engineering (Amasyali and El-Gohary, 2018; Wei et al., 2018). Neural networks and hybrid models appear when large samples or temporal sequences are available, though tabular EPCs often favour trees.

UK EPCs provide standardised fields for ratings, modelled consumption, construction descriptions, and heating fuels under the Open Government Licence. Prior studies use EPCs for stock characterisation, fuel poverty proxies, retrofit targeting, and rating prediction. Wenninger et al. (as discussed in Manfren et al., 2024) illustrate SHAP-based analysis of England and Wales EPC interventions linking fabric measures to emissions and socio-demographic context—showing that certificate data can support policy-facing interpretation when explanations are reported carefully. Fewer works isolate **high-rise** typologies with explicit storey or floor-area filters, and fewer still confront the mismatch between documentary high-rise thresholds and bulk export field quality. This dissertation contributes an audit-informed filter design that privileges honesty about data limitations over aspirational thresholds that empty the sample.

Recent interpretable modelling work continues to emphasise SHAP for residential energy performance assessment. For example, data-driven XGBoost-plus-SHAP pipelines on UK residential stock report very high apparent R² when rich rating-related fields are retained (Sustainability, 2026 synthesis of EPC assessment models), reinforcing the leakage risk already noted in this project: near-tautological features can inflate accuracy and must be excluded for scientific credibility.

## 2.2 Privacy-preserving learning for built-environment data

Energy and building datasets can be sensitive even when released under open licences: address-level linkages, small-area inference, and commercial HVAC configurations create residual risks. Privacy-preserving machine learning encompasses anonymisation, differential privacy, secure multiparty computation, and federated learning. FL is attractive when multiple organisations each hold locally rich but non-shareable tables—precisely the situation of local authorities and portfolio managers holding EPC-related or meter-linked datasets.

FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) iteratively averages client model parameters weighted by local dataset size. Under non-IID client distributions, client drift can degrade convergence; FedProx and related methods add proximal regularisation (Li et al., 2020). Flower (Beutel et al., 2020) provides an engineering framework for simulating and deploying FL.

**Recent FL applications to building energy (2024–2025).** Delgado Fernández et al. (2025) apply federated learning to >25,000 German residential buildings, simulating energy agencies as geographic clients. They report that FL improved prediction performance by approximately 12% relative to isolated local models while approaching a pooled central model—directly supporting the privacy–accuracy trade-off examined in RQ1. Khan et al. (2025) propose an FL-driven smart-building energy management architecture combined with XAI to address centralised data risk and opaque AI decisions in DER-rich settings. Attention-based deep models integrated with FL and SHAP for smart-grid load forecasting similarly argue that privacy and interpretability must be co-designed rather than bolted on (Intelligent Systems with Applications, 2024). Collectively, these studies show building-sector FL is no longer sparse, but they typically emphasise meters, load curves, or agency-held residential inventories rather than **UK high-rise EPC bulk certificates** with explicit explanation-stability metrics across centralised and federated regimes.

## 2.3 Explainable AI for regression on tabular data

Global explanations summarise which features drive predictions across a dataset; local explanations justify individual instances. SHAP assigns each feature an additive contribution based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, with desirable consistency properties (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). TreeExplainer computes exact SHAP values efficiently for tree ensembles; KernelExplainer approximates SHAP for arbitrary models at higher computational cost. LIME fits interpretable local surrogates around an instance (Ribeiro et al., 2016). Critiques of XAI emphasise fidelity, stability under perturbation, and the risk of misleading attributions (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017; Slack et al., 2020).

**Recent building-energy XAI (2024–2025).** Systematic reviews of XAI in smart buildings identify SHAP as the dominant post-hoc method for energy-saving and consumption models (Journal of Building Engineering, 2025). Empirical studies use SHAP to explain household and typology-level predictors in residential forecasting (Cui et al., 2024; Energy and Buildings) and to support adaptive learning pipelines that cluster explanations (Clement et al., 2024). Clustered FL with SHAP weighting has been proposed for solar forecasting (XCFL; Energies, 2025) and residential multivariate load forecasting (EXFEDL; 2026), showing that explainability can enter the aggregation loop itself—not only post-training dashboards. In federated settings, an additional question therefore arises: do explanations of a model trained without data pooling remain aligned with explanations of a centrally trained counterpart? This dissertation operationalises that question via Spearman correlation of mean absolute SHAP profiles (H2), while treating RF–federated comparison charts as a stricter cross-family check rather than the primary stability test.

## 2.4 Regulation, transparency, and the built environment

GDPR constrains processing of personal data and influences how household energy information may be shared. Commercial landlords may treat operational energy and system details as confidential. The EU AI Act (2024) elevates transparency and human oversight; Article 13 expectations motivate human-readable explanations even when research prototypes are not formally classified as high-risk systems. The EPBD recast (2024) likewise emphasises standardised, transparent assessment methodologies. While EPC prediction for research may not map one-to-one onto regulated high-risk categories, the **normative** argument remains: if AI informs retrofit advice or portfolio decisions, stakeholders benefit from explanations that are stable and auditable. PP-XAI therefore treats FL and XAI as complementary pillars of trustworthy analytics rather than optional extras.

## 2.5 Tabular learning for energy intensity

Cross-sectional prediction of energy intensity differs from short-term load forecasting. The former emphasises static building descriptors; the latter emphasises calendars, weather, and autoregression. EPC modelling belongs primarily to the former. Feature sets typically include floor area, construction age or age band, wall/roof/window descriptions, main heating fuel, and typology. Target variables may be modelled consumption (kWh/m²/year), primary energy, or ordinal rating bands. Classification of EPC bands is common in the literature; this dissertation focuses on continuous regression because federated averaging and SHAP analyses are more directly comparable on a shared numeric target.

Tree ensembles remain strong baselines on medium-sized tabular datasets. They handle mixed types after encoding, tolerate moderate missingness with imputation, and provide impurity-based importance that can be contrasted with SHAP. Neural MLPs require careful scaling and often more tuning; they are, however, natural citizens of FedAvg because parameter tensors average cleanly. Choosing an MLP as the federated model and trees as centralised competitors therefore reflects both methodological fairness and FL practicality (Chen et al., 2023).

## 2.6 Related work synthesis and research gap

Synthesising the above: (i) tabular ML on EPCs is mature for centralised prediction; (ii) FL for building energy is now empirically demonstrated in European residential and smart-building settings (Delgado Fernández et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025); (iii) SHAP/LIME are established—and increasingly standard—for building-energy regression (2024–2025 reviews and applications); (iv) integrated evaluation of **accuracy + explanation stability** for **UK high-rise EPC** federated learning remains lacking. No identified study under this review protocol simultaneously (a) revises high-rise filters against UK bulk schema reality, (b) compares centralised ensembles with FedAvg on London/Manchester/Birmingham partitions, and (c) reports Spearman stability of SHAP rankings across those regimes. Chapter 3 designs a methodology to address this gap within a constrained dissertation timeline.

## 2.7 Summary

The literature supports tree and neural models for EPC-like tabular prediction, FedAvg for cross-silo collaboration, and SHAP/LIME for explanation. Since 2024, FL+XAI work in buildings and grids has accelerated, increasing the significance of privacy-preserving explainable analytics—but UK high-rise EPC bulk data with audited filters and quantitative explanation stability across training regimes remains an open niche. Chapter 3 turns that gap into a concrete protocol.
